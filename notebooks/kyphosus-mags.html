Published

Paper: Oliver et al. 2024, mBio Code: KyphosusMAGs Data: MAGs on Zenodo SRA PRJNA819194 / PRJNA1023379

Herbivorous Kyphosus fish (nenue, or rudderfish) live on red, green and brown seaweed and rely on gut microbes to break down its polysaccharides. Algal glycans such as agar, carrageenan, alginate, fucoidan and ulvan are heavily sulfated, so the industrial enzymes used for land plants work poorly on them. We recovered metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from wild fish guts and from bioreactors seeded with gut contents, and identified which microbes carry the enzymes for each step of degradation.

This notebook rebuilds the main analyses from the processed tables in the repository. Most figures are interactive: hover for values, click legend entries to hide groups, and drag to zoom.

4 Short-chain fatty acid production

The fish get much of their energy from short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) made by fermentation. gutSMASH was used to find the pathways for each SCFA. Bubble size is the percentage of MAGs in a lineage that carry the pathway.

Code 
scfa <- read.delim(kd("scfa_breakdown.tab"), check.names = FALSE) %>%
  pivot_longer(-Taxa, names_to = "Pathway", values_to = "Fraction") %>%
  mutate(Pathway = str_trim(Pathway),
         Product = case_when(str_detect(Pathway, "Butyr|Lysine") ~ "Butyrate",
                             str_detect(Pathway, "Propion") ~ "Propanoate",
                             TRUE ~ "Acetate"),
         Pathway = str_replace_all(Pathway, "Propionate", "Propanoate"),
         # sentence case for product names inside a pathway name ("Pyruvate to Acetate" -> "Pyruvate to acetate")
         Pathway = str_replace_all(Pathway, "(?<=.)\\b(Acetate|Propanoate|Butyrate|Formate)", tolower),
         Pathway = factor(Pathway, levels = unique(Pathway)),

5 Similarity to reference enzymes

Each binned CAZyme and sulfatase was compared against three references: other MAGs in this study, GenBank nr, and the curated CAZy or SulfAtlas databases. Across all annotated genes, the median best-hit identity is 63% to GenBank nr, 56% to CAZy and 36% to SulfAtlas. Most are distant from characterized enzymes, so the Kyphosus gut is a likely source of new seaweed-degrading enzymes.

The plot shows the five CAZyme and five sulfatase families (from the algal-polysaccharide families analyzed in the paper) whose genes are least similar to anything in GenBank nr. Their median best-hit identity is about 40 to 60%.

least_similar <- function(fams) {

6 A GH86 β-agarase/β-porphyranase with an undescribed domain

GH86 enzymes cut agar and porphyran, the main glycans of red algae. The gene tree of binned GH86 sequences has a clade from three different phyla (Bacillota, Bacteroidota and Verrucomicrobiota) that share an N-terminal region with no characterized function. Most of these sequences also have a signal peptide, so they are likely exported. This is consistent with several taxa breaking these glycans down outside the cell.

7 Biosynthetic gene clusters

antiSMASH found 307 biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) in the MAGs. BiG-SLiCE then measured each one’s distance to the nearest known gene cluster family (GCF). Clusters above the dotted line at a distance of 900 are counted as novel.

The sunburst groups the clusters by antiSMASH class (inner ring) and product type (outer ring). Click a class to zoom in.

Herbivorous *Kyphosus* fish (nenue, or rudderfish) live on red, green and brown
seaweed and rely on gut microbes to break down its polysaccharides. Algal
glycans such as agar, carrageenan, alginate, fucoidan and ulvan are heavily
sulfated, so the industrial enzymes used for land plants work poorly on them.
We recovered metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from wild fish guts and from
bioreactors seeded with gut contents, and identified which microbes carry the
enzymes for each step of degradation.

This notebook rebuilds the main analyses from the processed tables in the
repository. Most figures are interactive: hover for values, click legend entries
to hide groups, and drag to zoom.

```{r load-data}
bin_taxa    <- read.delim(kd("bin_taxa.tab"))
bin_cazy    <- read.delim(kd("bin_cazy.tab"), check.names = FALSE)
bin_source  <- read.delim(kd("bin_source.tab"))
bin_scfa    <- read.delim(kd("bin_scfa.tab"))
bin_sulf    <- read.delim(kd("bin_sulf_reduct.tab"), check.names = FALSE)
bin_bgc     <- read.delim(kd("bin_bgc.tab"), check.names = FALSE)
bin_quality <- read.delim(kd("bin_quality.txt"))

  rename(MAG = bin, Taxonomy = taxa, `Genome size (bp)` = size,
         CGCs = cgcs, `CGC genes` = cgcgenes) %>%
  left_join(bin_cazy %>% rename(MAG = bins), by = "MAG") %>%
  left_join(bin_source %>% select(MAG = bins, Source), by = "MAG") %>%
  left_join(bin_scfa %>% select(MAG = MAG_id, Acetate, Propanoate, Butyrate), by = "MAG") %>%

  left_join(bin_bgc %>% count(MAG, name = "BGCs"), by = "MAG") %>%
  mutate(BGCs = replace_na(BGCs, 0))
```

## Short-chain fatty acid production

The fish get much of their energy from short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) made by
fermentation. gutSMASH was used to find the pathways for each SCFA. Bubble size
is the percentage of MAGs in a lineage that carry the pathway.

```{r scfa}

  pivot_longer(-Taxa, names_to = "Pathway", values_to = "Fraction") %>%
  mutate(Pathway = str_trim(Pathway),

                             str_detect(Pathway, "Propion") ~ "Propanoate",
                             TRUE ~ "Acetate"),
```

## Similarity to reference enzymes

Each binned CAZyme and sulfatase was compared against three references: other
MAGs in this study, GenBank nr, and the curated CAZy or SulfAtlas databases.
Across all annotated genes, the median best-hit identity is 63% to GenBank nr,
56% to CAZy and 36% to SulfAtlas. Most are distant from characterized
enzymes, so the *Kyphosus* gut is a likely source of new seaweed-degrading
enzymes.

The plot shows the five CAZyme and five sulfatase families (from the
algal-polysaccharide families analyzed in the paper) whose genes are least
similar to anything in GenBank nr. Their median best-hit identity is about
40 to 60%.

## A GH86 β-agarase/β-porphyranase with an undescribed domain

GH86 enzymes cut agar and porphyran, the main glycans of red algae. The gene
tree of binned GH86 sequences has a clade from three different phyla
(Bacillota, Bacteroidota and Verrucomicrobiota) that share an N-terminal region
with no characterized function. Most of these sequences also have a signal
peptide, so they are likely exported. This is consistent with several taxa
breaking these glycans down outside the cell.

## Biosynthetic gene clusters

BiG-SLiCE then measured each one's distance to the nearest known gene cluster
family (GCF). Clusters above the dotted line at a distance of 900 are counted as
novel.

The sunburst groups the clusters by antiSMASH class (inner ring) and product
